The Trade Already Has Structured Data

Specialist trades spent a century standardising identification. Most projects ignore it and invent a taxonomy from scratch.

Published 25 July 2026 · 5 min read · Anchored to Panama Treasures

Watches. Gemstones. Art. Marine. Aviation. Rare books. What these categories share is not price but a problem each solved long ago: proving that this specific object is what someone claims it is. Solving it produced identification standards of real rigour — reference numbers, serials, hallmarks, certificates, provenance chains, condition grading. That is structured data, and it predates the web by decades.

Structured data needs stable identifiers, defined attributes and controlled vocabulary. Most businesses lack all three, which is why the standard advice is to build them. Specialist trades are the exception, and theirs is enforced by a stricter mechanism than any style guide: if the identifiers are wrong, the transaction does not clear. The task is not inventing a taxonomy but mapping one that is already maintained and already agreed across the whole trade.

That is a smaller job and a better outcome, because the resulting vocabulary matches what buyers actually search with. A collector does not search for “luxury timepiece” — they search a reference number. Generic schema advice is written for catalogue retail and assumes one model with many identical units; applied to one-of-one inventory it describes the model and quietly discards everything that makes the individual object worth its price. The markup validates. It describes the wrong thing.
