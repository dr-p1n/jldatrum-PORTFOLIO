Instruments Beat Pitches

A pitch asks someone to accept your framing. An instrument lets them measure themselves and arrive at it alone.

Published 25 July 2026 · 4 min read · Anchored to the instruments on this site

There is a moment in most sales conversations where you explain why the thing you noticed matters. You have the diagnosis, it is correct, and you are asking someone to take your word for it.

They rarely do. Not because they doubt you specifically, but because you are the party who benefits from the diagnosis being true. That is not cynicism on their part. It is basic hygiene.

The mechanism

A pitch and an instrument make the same claim through structurally different routes.

A pitch asserts: here is a problem you have, and here is why it matters. Its credibility depends entirely on the credibility of the asserter, who is also the vendor. The prospect must resolve a conflict of interest before they can even evaluate the content.

An instrument does something else. It hands over the criteria and lets the prospect apply them to their own situation. The conclusion is produced by their inputs, so its authority does not depend on trusting you at all.

A conclusion someone reaches by measuring themselves is not a claim they have to accept. It is a result they already own.

The interesting consequence is what happens to the vendor’s position. After an instrument, you are no longer the person asserting a problem. You are the person who supplied the ruler — which is a considerably better place to be standing when the conversation turns to what to do about it.

Why it has to be genuinely useful

This only works if the instrument is real, and there are two ways to make it fake.

The first is rigging the scale so everyone lands in a band that requires your help. Anyone who runs it against a site they know is well built will notice immediately, and the credibility loss is larger than whatever the instrument earned.

The second is gating it. A scored diagnostic behind an email form is not an instrument; it is a lead-capture mechanism wearing one as a costume. It also breaks the thing that made it work — the prospect no longer feels they measured themselves, because they paid for the ruler with their contact details before seeing a number.

An instrument has to be able to return a good score. It has to be able to tell someone they are fine. If it cannot do both, it is a pitch with arithmetic.

What it actually looks like

This piece is self-referential, so it is fair to check it against its own standard.

There are three instruments on this site. A Ley 81 compliance checklist scoring out of six. A security header reference. A visibility self-diagnostic scoring out of twenty-five. All three return a real result, all three can return a good one, and none of them asks for an email address at any point.

They are not advertisements for the practice. They are the same diagnostic work the practice does, published in a form that runs without me. If someone uses all three, fixes what they find, and never makes contact, the instruments still did their job.

That is not generosity. It is the mechanism working: most people who run an honest diagnostic on their own site find more than they expected, and the ones who want help now want it on the basis of something they measured rather than something they were told.

The diagnostic

Look at whatever you currently send a prospect who is not yet convinced — the deck, the capability statement, the case study PDF.

Does it ask them to believe something, or does it let them find something out?

If it only does the first, there is usually a version that does the second, built from criteria you already apply. It is more work to produce and it converts differently — slower at the top, considerably warmer at the bottom.
